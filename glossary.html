Collecting data

Sample and population

The population is the whole group you want to say something about, for example all players of this kind of game. The sample is the smaller group you actually measure, here the students in this class.

ExampleOur class of 20 is a sample. We hope it tells us something about how similar players would experience the game.

In this tool: Every statistic on the Results page describes the sample. The confidence interval is the tool's attempt to say something about the population.

Participant and participant number

A participant is one person who takes part in a study. A participant number is an anonymous code used instead of a name, so the data cannot be linked to an individual but a response can still be found and corrected.

In this tool: Each row on the Data page is one participant, identified only by number (P1, P2 and so on).

n (sample size)

n is the number of observations, usually the number of participants whose data are included in a calculation. Statisticians write a lower-case n for a sample.

ExampleIf 18 students submit the survey, n = 18. If one suspicious response is excluded, n becomes 17.

In this tool: n is shown first in every statistics row on the Results page. Small n means less certainty: the confidence interval gets wider and the results can change noticeably when one more person responds.

Variable

A variable is anything that is measured and can take different values across participants. Each UEQ-S item is a variable, and so is each dimension score.

In this tool: The Data page has one column per variable: Q1 to Q8, then PQ, HQ and Overall.

Standardised questionnaire

A standardised questionnaire uses fixed items, wording, answer scales and scoring rules that have been tested in earlier research. Because everyone uses it the same way, results can be compared across studies and products.

In this tool: The UEQ-S is a standardised questionnaire with 8 items. Changing the wording or the order of items would break the comparison with the benchmark.

Semantic differential

A semantic differential is an answer format in which a person places their impression on a line between two opposite words, such as boring and exciting.

In this tool: All 8 UEQ-S items are semantic differentials with 7 points. The negative word is always on the left (1) and the positive word on the right (7).

Item

An item is a single question or rating in a questionnaire.

In this tool: The UEQ-S has 8 items. Items 1 to 4 measure pragmatic quality and items 5 to 8 measure hedonic quality.

Levels of measurement (ordinal and interval data)

Ordinal data have a clear order but the gaps between values are not guaranteed to be equal (for example 1st, 2nd and 3rd place). Interval data have equal gaps (for example temperature in °C). Strictly speaking, a single rating on a 7-point scale is ordinal.

In this tool: Like most questionnaire research, the UEQ treats the averaged item scores as approximately interval, which is why it reports means. This is common practice, but it is a reason to check the median as well.

Preparing data

Raw score and re-coding

A raw score is the value exactly as the participant entered it. Re-coding changes raw values into a form that is easier to analyse or interpret.

How it is calculatedRe-coded score = raw score − 4
ExampleA raw answer of 7 becomes +3, 4 becomes 0, and 1 becomes −3.

In this tool: Switch between Raw (1–7) and Scored (−3 to +3) on the Data page to see both versions.

Dimension (scale score)

A dimension (also called a scale or construct) is an underlying quality that cannot be measured directly with one question, so several items are combined. A participant's scale score is the mean of their re-coded answers to those items.

How it is calculatedPragmatic quality = (Q1 + Q2 + Q3 + Q4) ÷ 4, using re-coded values
ExampleA participant who answers 6, 5, 6, 7 on items 1 to 4 has re-coded values 2, 1, 2, 3. Their pragmatic quality score is (2 + 1 + 2 + 3) ÷ 4 = 2.00.

In this tool: The UEQ-S has two dimensions, pragmatic quality (useful, easy, efficient, clear) and hedonic quality (exciting, interesting, inventive, leading edge), plus an overall score from all 8 items.

Data cleaning

Data cleaning means checking data for errors, duplicates or implausible answers before analysis, and deciding (and reporting) what to do about them.

In this tool: The survey prevents missing answers and asks before a participant number is reused. The Results page flags suspicious response patterns and lets you see what happens if they are excluded.

Inconsistent or suspicious response

A response pattern that is unlikely to reflect a genuine opinion, for example when someone answers at random or does not read the items.

How it is calculatedFlag a dimension when (highest − lowest re-coded item value) > 3. Suspicious = flagged in both dimensions.
ExampleAnswers of +3, −3, +2 and −2 on the four pragmatic items differ by 6 points. The items are meant to measure the same thing, so this pattern is unlikely to be genuine.

In this tool: Flagged cells are red on the Data page, and the Results page has an Exclude suspicious responses option.

Describing data

Descriptive and inferential statistics

Descriptive statistics summarise the data you collected (mean, median, standard deviation). Inferential statistics use the sample to make careful statements about the wider population (confidence intervals, significance tests).

In this tool: Most of the Results page is descriptive. The 95% confidence interval is inferential.

Distribution

The distribution of a variable is the pattern of how its values are spread out: where most values lie, how far they spread, and whether the pattern is symmetrical.

In this tool: The dots in the Participants row of each chart show the whole distribution, one dot per person.

Mean (average)

The mean is the sum of all values divided by the number of values. It uses every data point, so a few very high or very low values can pull it up or down.

How it is calculatedMean = sum of values ÷ n
ExampleScores −1, 0.5, 1, 1.5 and 3 add up to 5. With n = 5, the mean is 5 ÷ 5 = 1.00.

In this tool: The diamond in each chart marks the mean. The UEQ benchmark and the ±0.8 rule are based on the mean.

Median

The median is the middle value when all values are sorted from lowest to highest. Half of the values are at or below it, half at or above. It is not affected by extreme values, which makes it robust.

How it is calculatedSort the values. If n is odd, take the middle one. If n is even, take the mean of the two middle ones.
ExampleSorted scores −1, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 3: the median is 1. If the 3 were changed to 30, the mean would jump to 6.4 but the median would still be 1.

In this tool: The thick vertical line inside each box is the median.

Mode

The mode is the value that occurs most often. It is most useful for single items or categories.

ExampleIf seven students choose 6 for boring–exciting and fewer choose any other value, the mode of that item is 6.

In this tool: Not shown in the tool, but you can spot it in the Data page or as the tallest stack of dots in a chart.

Minimum, maximum and range

The minimum and maximum are the lowest and highest values. The range is the difference between them. Because it depends on only two values, it is very sensitive to a single unusual response.

How it is calculatedRange = maximum − minimum
ExampleFor −1, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 3 the range is 3 − (−1) = 4.

In this tool: The report under each chart states the range of individual scores.

Quartiles (Q1 and Q3)

Quartiles split sorted data into four equal parts. The first quartile (Q1) has 25% of values below it, the median is the second quartile, and the third quartile (Q3) has 75% of values below it.

How it is calculatedThis tool uses linear interpolation between sorted values, the same method as Excel's QUARTILE.INC.
ExampleFor −1, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 3: Q1 = 0.5 and Q3 = 1.5.

In this tool: Q1 and Q3 are the left and right edges of each box.

Interquartile range (IQR)

The interquartile range is the spread of the middle 50% of the data. Unlike the range, it ignores the most extreme quarter at each end, so it is robust to outliers.

How it is calculatedIQR = Q3 − Q1
ExampleWith Q1 = 0.5 and Q3 = 1.5, the IQR is 1.0.

In this tool: The IQR is the width of the box. A narrow box means the middle half of the class largely agreed.

Variance

The variance measures how far values are from the mean on average, using squared distances. It is the step before the standard deviation and is used inside Cronbach's alpha.

How it is calculatedVariance = sum of (value − mean)² ÷ (n − 1)
ExampleFor −1, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 3 (mean 1): the distances are −2, −0.5, 0, 0.5, 2; squared 4, 0.25, 0, 0.25, 4; sum 8.5. Variance = 8.5 ÷ 4 = 2.125.

Standard deviation (SD)

The standard deviation describes the typical distance of values from the mean, in the same units as the data. A small SD means people answered similarly. A large SD means opinions were spread out.

How it is calculatedSD = √variance
ExampleWith a variance of 2.125, the SD is √2.125 ≈ 1.46. On the UEQ scale this is a large spread: participants differ by about one and a half points from the mean on average.

In this tool: The report uses rough guides for UEQ data: SD below 0.6 suggests strong agreement, 0.6 to 1.2 is typical, above 1.2 means opinions differed widely. We divide by n − 1 rather than n because we are estimating the spread of the population from a sample.

Skew (skewed distribution)

A distribution is skewed when it is not symmetrical, with a longer tail on one side. A few very low scores create a long tail to the left and pull the mean below the median.

ExampleIf most students score around +2 but two score −2, the mean drops below the median.

In this tool: The report warns when the mean and median differ by 0.3 or more. In the chart, look for a median that is off-centre in its box or one whisker much longer than the other.

Visualising data

Box plot

A box plot (box-and-whisker plot) summarises a distribution with five numbers: the lowest and highest non-outlier values, Q1, the median and Q3. It makes it easy to compare the centre and spread of several groups.

In this tool: Each dimension on the Results page has its own box plot on the same −3 to +3 axis, so they can be compared directly.

Whiskers

The whiskers are the lines extending from the box. They reach to the lowest and highest values that are not outliers.

How it is calculatedWhiskers stop at the furthest data point within 1.5 × IQR of the box.

Outlier

An outlier is a value unusually far from the rest of the data. An outlier is not automatically an error: it may be a person with a genuinely different experience, which is often worth discussing.

How it is calculatedOutlier if value < Q1 − 1.5 × IQR, or value > Q3 + 1.5 × IQR
ExampleWith Q1 = 0.5, Q3 = 1.5 and IQR = 1, the fences are −1 and 3. A score of −1.25 would be an outlier, while −1 and 3 sit exactly on the fences and are not.

In this tool: Outliers are drawn as hollow circles in the box plot row and named in the report.

Inference

Standard error (SE)

The standard error estimates how much the sample mean would vary if the study were repeated many times with different samples. It shrinks as the sample gets larger.

How it is calculatedSE = SD ÷ √n
ExampleWith SD = 1.46 and n = 5, SE = 1.46 ÷ √5 ≈ 0.65. With the same SD and n = 20, SE ≈ 0.33.

In this tool: Not shown separately, but it is the building block of the confidence interval.

95% confidence interval (CI)

A confidence interval is a range of plausible values for the population mean, based on the sample. A 95% CI is built with a method that captures the true mean in 95% of studies. It does not mean the class's own scores fall inside it.

How it is calculated95% CI = mean ± t × SE
ExampleMean 1.00, SE 0.65, n = 5 (t = 2.78): 1.00 ± 1.81, so the CI runs from −0.81 to 2.81. That is very wide: with only five people we cannot even be sure the evaluation is positive.

In this tool: Shown as the horizontal bar with end caps in the Mean ± 95% CI row. When the bar crosses a boundary (such as +0.8 or a benchmark category), the report says the result is uncertain.

t value (t distribution)

The t value is the multiplier used to build a confidence interval when the sample is small and the population SD is unknown. It is larger for small samples, which widens the interval to reflect the extra uncertainty.

ExampleFor 95% confidence: t ≈ 2.78 when n = 5, 2.26 when n = 10, 2.09 when n = 20, and it approaches 1.96 in very large samples.

Paired comparison

A paired comparison compares two measurements taken from the same people. Because each person is compared with themselves, differences between people (for example someone who rates everything high) cancel out.

How it is calculatedDifference for each participant = HQ − PQ, then take the mean of these differences

In this tool: The Comparing the two dimensions card counts how many participants rated hedonic quality higher than pragmatic quality, and the reverse.

Statistical significance (p value)

A result is statistically significant when it would be unlikely to occur by chance if there were really no effect. The p value gives that probability; p < 0.05 is a common threshold. Significance does not tell you whether a difference is large or important.

In this tool: The tool does not run significance tests. A rough visual guide: if a confidence interval does not include a value (for example 0 or +0.8), the mean is likely to differ from it.

Quality of measurement

Reliability and validity

Reliability is whether an instrument measures consistently: would people get similar scores if measured again, and do items meant to measure the same thing agree? Validity is whether it measures what it claims to measure. A ruler that always reads 2 cm too long is reliable but not valid.

In this tool: The UEQ-S has been validated in published studies. Cronbach's alpha gives a quick reliability check for our class data.

Cronbach's alpha (α)

Cronbach's alpha measures internal consistency: how strongly the items within one dimension agree with each other across participants. If people who rate the game as easy also tend to rate it as clear, efficient and supportive, alpha is high. Alpha usually ranges from 0 to 1, and can be negative if items disagree badly.

How it is calculatedα = k ÷ (k − 1) × (1 − sum of item variances ÷ variance of participants' item totals), where k = number of items (4 for each UEQ-S dimension)
ExampleRules of thumb: 0.9 and above excellent, 0.8 good, 0.7 acceptable, 0.6 questionable, below 0.6 poor. Alpha needs at least 3 participants and is unstable in small samples, so a value of 0.55 from 12 students should be read with caution.

In this tool: Reported for pragmatic and hedonic quality on the Results page (not for Overall, which mixes the two). A low alpha in a game study can also mean some items read oddly for games, for example efficient. The Item means chart can show which item behaves differently.

Interpreting UEQ results

Neutral band (±0.8)

The UEQ authors suggest reading dimension means between −0.8 and +0.8 as a neutral evaluation, above +0.8 as positive and below −0.8 as negative. Means beyond ±2 are rare because people tend to avoid the extreme ends of a scale.

In this tool: Shown as the shaded grey area in every chart.

Benchmark

A benchmark is a reference data set used to judge whether a result is good or bad compared with others. The UEQ-S benchmark contains 9,905 responses evaluating 246 interactive products, grouped into five categories from Bad (bottom 25%) to Excellent (top 10%).

ExampleA pragmatic quality mean of 1.30 falls in Above average: 25% of benchmark products scored higher and 50% scored lower.

In this tool: Each dimension card on the Results page has a benchmark table with the category of the class mean highlighted. Shaded rows show the categories covered by the confidence interval.

Practical importance

A difference can be real but too small to matter, or large but uncertain because the sample is small. Always look at the size of a difference and its confidence interval together, and ask what it would mean for the design.

ExampleA 0.1 difference between two games on a −3 to +3 scale is unlikely to matter to players, even with a large sample.
